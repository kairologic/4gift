When a friend's dog or cat dies, the kindest thing you can give is acknowledgment. A gift can help carry it, but the words come first. Here's the short list, by timing:

- **The first days:** a card or a short message that uses the pet's name. Nothing to unwrap, nothing to reply to.
- **The weeks after:** a meal, a walk together, or an hour of your time to just listen.
- **When they're ready:** something that keeps the memory close, like a portrait from a favorite photo or a plant to put in the ground.
- **The months later:** a note on a day that mattered, because grief comes back.
- **Leave unsaid:** "it was only a cat" and "you could get another one."

## Why does losing a pet so often go unacknowledged?

<!-- visual: callout | Disenfranchised grief: a real loss that others don't treat as one -->

Because many people don't treat it as a real loss. Ohio State University's Veterinary Medical Center calls pet loss a form of disenfranchised grief: society still doesn't recognize it as a "valid" form of grief, so the support people would normally expect may not be there.

The people who answer the phones see this every week. Tufts University's Cummings School of Veterinary Medicine has run a Pet Loss Support Hotline, staffed by veterinary students, since 1996. Tufts Now reported that callers often say they can't talk about their grief with the people in their lives, and that some have been told "it's only a cat" or "just get another dog."

So the gap is rarely a missing object. It's that nobody said anything. That is the part you can fix.

## What should you give in the first few days?

A card, or a few words in a message. Use the pet's name, and say one specific thing you remember about them, if you have one.

It sounds small, and it isn't. Ohio State's guide suggests that grieving owners save the sympathy cards and condolence emails they receive, and even use them to make a collage or poster. A card is one of the very few gifts the guide names, and it's the one most likely to be kept.

In these first days, Ohio State notes, grief can be exhausting, physically, emotionally and spiritually, with changes in sleep and appetite. So keep your message free of questions they have to answer. "Thinking of you and Biscuit today" asks nothing back.

## What helps in the weeks that follow?

Your time, more than a thing. Ohio State's guide says that talking about the loss with people who will understand helps owners work through it, and it lists reaching out to supportive friends as part of grieving well.

A few ways to offer that, with a real date attached:

- **A meal** dropped at the door, so the evening routine that used to include the pet has one less empty slot.
- **A walk** at a new time or on a new route. The guide suggests that owners who walked their dog may want to walk at a different time and place at first.
- **An hour to listen.** Tufts students say callers often just want someone to hear them. You don't need the right words.

Guilt is common, too. One student volunteer told Tufts Now that nearly all of her callers feel some guilt about their loss, often that they acted too soon or waited too long. If your friend says this, you don't have to argue with it. Listening is enough.

## When is a keepsake the right gift?

<!-- visual: table | 3 kinds of keepsake, what each keeps, and when to give it -->

When they're ready to remember rather than only to miss. Ohio State's guide calls ritual important after a loss and lists ways owners find helpful, including a memory box, a journal of the pet's story, planting something living, and commissioned portraits.

| Keepsake | What it keeps | A gentle way to give it |
|---|---|---|
| Custom pet portrait | Their face, as you knew them | Ask for a favorite photo, or use one you took |
| Memory box or frame | Collar, tags, a paw print, photos | Give it empty; they fill it when they want |
| A plant or small tree | A living place to remember | Offer to help plant it, on a day they choose |

A custom portrait is the one I'd think hardest about. It works best from a photo the owner loves, so it's fine to ask for one. If asking feels too soon, wait. A memory box holds what the owner already has, like the collar, tags, a paw print and photos, so it never feels like a replacement. And a plant gives them somewhere to go.

There's no rush on any of these. A keepsake given a month or two later can land more softly than one that arrives the same week.

## What should you avoid saying or giving?

<!-- visual: callout | 3 things to skip: shrinking the loss, a new pet, a fixed timeline -->

Anything that makes the loss smaller or hurries it along.

- **Shrinking it.** "It was only a cat" and "it was just a dog" are the remarks Tufts callers say hurt.
- **A new pet.** Ohio State's guide says not to rush into adopting as a way to ease grief, and that a new pet too soon can imply grief is unimportant. It notes that a new animal never replaces the one who died. That choice is the owner's, in their own time.
- **A deadline.** The guide says grief follows no plan or schedule, and that there is no standard duration for it. Avoid "you should be feeling better by now."

If there are children in the house, the guide adds that their grief can resurface later, even well after the death. A small note to the child, using the pet's name, can mean a lot.

## When should someone get more support?

If sorrow or guilt hasn't eased after several weeks, or it's making work, family or daily life hard, Ohio State's guide suggests a trained mental health professional. It also says many people find comfort in a pet loss support hotline or group, and veterinary schools such as Tufts run them. Mentioning that such lines exist is a kind thing to do. It tells your friend their grief is the kind people take seriously.

## So what would I give?

A card first, in the first few days, with the pet's name in it. Then, a few weeks on, a meal or an hour to listen.

Later, when they're ready, I'd give a custom portrait from a photo they love, or a memory box they can fill themselves. And I'd write again on a date that mattered, like the pet's birthday or the day they came home, because, as Ohio State puts it, grief follows no schedule.

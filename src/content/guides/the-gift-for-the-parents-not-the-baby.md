The baby will get blankets, onesies and a stuffed animal or six. The parents usually get nothing, and they're the ones up at 3 a.m. Here's the short version:

- **Give food:** prepaid meals, spread over several weeks rather than all landing in week one.
- **Give hours:** a block of help with a date on it, from you or someone they hire with your gift.
- **Give comfort:** something for the parent alone, like a plush robe, that has nothing to do with the baby.
- **Time it later than you think.** Visits tend to bunch up in the first days. ACOG's own timeline runs to 12 weeks.

This is a guide to gifts, not medical advice. Questions about a new parent's health belong with their ob-gyn.

## What does ACOG say about the weeks after birth?

<!-- visual: chart | ACOG timeline: contact within the first 3 weeks, ongoing care, comprehensive visit no later than 12 weeks -->

That they matter a great deal, and for longer than one checkup. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG), in its Committee Opinion No. 736, calls the weeks following birth "a critical period for a woman and her infant."

ACOG revised that opinion in 2018, and reaffirmed it in 2025, to stress what it calls the "fourth trimester." Its point is that postpartum care should become an ongoing process rather than a single encounter. It recommends contact with an ob-gyn or other obstetric care provider within the first 3 weeks, then ongoing care as needed, ending with a comprehensive visit no later than 12 weeks after birth.

What's on that comprehensive visit is telling for a gift-giver. Alongside mood, feeding and physical recovery, ACOG lists "sleep and fatigue" as one of the areas to assess. Doctors are told to ask about tiredness. The rest of us can do something about it.

## Why is tiredness the gap a gift can fill?

Because it lasts, and because it's practical. ACOG's patient FAQ on postpartum depression says many people feel very tired after giving birth, and that it can take weeks to regain strength and energy. Recovery can take even longer after a cesarean birth, it adds.

The same FAQ names fatigue as a possible major contributor to postpartum depression, and it lists lack of support from others among the lifestyle factors that can greatly increase the risk. A gift can't treat anything, and it shouldn't pretend to. But a gift can be support: a meal nobody had to cook, an hour nobody had to find.

So the useful question isn't "what does the baby need?" It's "what would let this parent sit down?"

## When should the help arrive?

<!-- visual: table | 3 stretches of ACOG's postpartum timeline, each with a gift that fits -->

Later, and spread out. ACOG's own timeline runs to 12 weeks, and its FAQ says postpartum depression most commonly starts about 1 to 3 weeks after childbirth, after the baby blues, which it says usually get better within a few days or 1 to 2 weeks.

That suggests a gift that keeps arriving:

| Stretch | What ACOG's timeline includes | A gift that fits |
|---|---|---|
| Weeks 1 to 3 | Contact with a care provider within the first 3 weeks | A dropped-off meal, a short visit |
| Weeks 3 to 12 | Ongoing care as needed | Prepaid meals, a block of help |
| By week 12 | A comprehensive visit, sleep and fatigue included | A night or afternoon off, a robe |

The first two columns are ACOG's; the third is ours. The point is simple: week five is just as hungry as week one, and often much quieter.

## What does a gift for the parents look like?

<!-- visual: callout | 3 gift types for the parents: prepaid meals, a block of help, a plush robe -->

Three kinds work, and each one is about the adult in the house.

1. **Prepaid meals, staggered.** A run of dinners from a local restaurant or a meal service, booked for weeks three through six instead of all at once. Ask about allergies and what they actually like to eat. If you'd rather cook, a freezer full of labeled portions does the same job.
2. **A block of help with a date on it.** Your own hours, written on a card: "Saturday the 14th, 1 to 5, I'll take the baby for a walk and do the dishes." Or, if you're far away, a gift toward hired help, such as a cleaning visit or an overnight helper. Let the parents choose who comes into their home.
3. **Something for the parent alone.** A plush bathrobe is a small, soft thing that belongs to them, not the nursery. It suits the 3 a.m. feed and the slow morning, and it's rarely the thing anyone thinks to add to a registry.

For more ideas sorted by who in the family they're for, our [gifts for new parents guide](/guides/gift-ideas-for-new-parents/) has a table, and the safe sleep rules for anything you'd put near the crib.

## How do you offer help without making it a chore?

Make it specific and make it easy to say no to. "Let me know if you need anything" hands the parent a job: figuring out what to ask for. A date, a time and a task hand them a yes or no.

A few rules keep help helpful:

- **Name the task.** Dinner, dishes, laundry, a walk with the baby, a ride for an older sibling.
- **Keep visits short.** Drop food at the door if that's what they want.
- **Bring it ready.** Food in containers they don't need to return.
- **Don't ask them to host you.** You're there to take a job off the list, not add one.

## What if they ask for baby things?

Then give baby things. A registry is a request, and honoring it is kind. You can add a card that promises one meal or one afternoon, so the parents get something too. A small gift for them alongside the registry item is often the one they mention later.

## So what would I give?

I'd give staggered meals for weeks three to six, paired with a card that names one afternoon of help with a real date. If I lived far away, I'd send a plush robe for the parent who's doing the night feeds, with a note that it's for them, not the baby.

ACOG asks doctors to check on sleep and fatigue for up to 12 weeks after birth. A gift that buys a nap, a hot dinner or a quiet hour is the one place the rest of us can help with that.

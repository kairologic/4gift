<!-- visual: callout | The finding in one line: givers expect surprises to win; recipients rate the requested gift as more thoughtful -->

Most of us believe a surprise shows more care than buying off a list. The research says the person opening the gift disagrees. Here's the short version:

- **What givers think:** a gift they picked themselves will seem more thoughtful than one the recipient asked for.
- **What recipients report:** they appreciate the requested gift more, and rate it as the more thoughtful one.
- **Where it holds best:** when the recipient names one specific thing, not a long list.
- **The twist:** recipients valued money more than the item they asked for, and givers guessed the opposite.
- **What to do:** give the request, then make it yours with a better version, a personal extra or a note.

## Where does this finding come from?

<!-- visual: table | 5 studies: wedding registries, birthday scenario, Amazon wish lists, single request, money -->

From Francesca Gino and Francis Flynn, who ran five experiments published in the *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology* in 2011 under the title "Give them what they want."

Stanford's summary of the paper lays out the studies. One asked people about real wedding gifts, from the registry or chosen freely. One had people imagine giving a spouse a birthday gift from a list or not. In a lab study, recipients built an Amazon wish list of ten items, and givers either picked from it or chose on their own. A fourth tested a single named request, and a fifth tested money.

The British Psychological Society's research digest gives the sizes for several of them: nearly 200 people in the wedding study, 160 in the birthday study, and 90 in the wish-list study.

Across the studies, the pattern was the same. Recipients appreciated what they had asked for more. Givers did not see it coming.

## Why does the surprise feel more thoughtful to the giver?

Because the giver knows how much effort went into it. The recipient mostly sees the result.

Stanford's write-up puts the gap plainly: the givers thought recipients would be more impressed with unsolicited items. The BPS digest adds that in the birthday study, recipients' extra appreciation for a requested gift came from feeling the giver had been extra thoughtful. Honoring a request reads as listening.

A later review by Jeff Galak, Julian Givi and Elanor Williams, in *Current Directions in Psychological Science* in 2016, folds this into a wider pattern. They argue givers judge a gift by the moment it's opened, while recipients judge it by how valuable it will be once they own it. A surprise is built for the moment. A request is built for the months after.

## Does a whole wish list work as well as one request?

Not quite. A single, specific request works better for givers.

Stanford's summary notes that givers seem more receptive to one specific suggestion than to a list. When the recipient named one gift, givers predicted the recipient's appreciation more accurately.

So if you're the one being asked what you want, name one thing. If you're the giver and you get a list, pick from it. Don't treat it as a starting point for a guess.

## What about just giving money?

<!-- visual: chart | Money vs requested item: what givers predicted vs what recipients said -->

Recipients liked it more than givers expected. In the final study, which the BPS digest says involved 107 students, givers thought money would be appreciated less than a requested item. Recipients said they would value the money more than the thing they had asked for.

That doesn't make cash the right answer every time. Some families and occasions treat money as cold, and you know your people better than a lab does. But if your only objection to cash is that it seems thoughtless, the recipients in this study didn't see it that way.

## How do you give the requested gift so it doesn't feel like a transaction?

<!-- visual: callout | 3 ways to make a requested gift personal without swapping it for a guess -->

Keep the request intact and add something around it. Three ways work without second-guessing what they asked for:

1. **Give the better version of the same thing.** If they asked for a chef's knife, a hand-forged one from a small bladesmith is still the gift they named. The Galak review notes that recipients prefer higher-quality gifts, because those give the most value once owned.
2. **Add a personal extra.** A personalized cutting board engraved with their initials, set under the knife they asked for, says you thought about it. The request stays the main gift.
3. **Write why.** A note that says "you said you wanted this, so here it is" turns the purchase into proof that you listened.

What to avoid: swapping their pick for something you think is "like it but better" in a different color, brand or style. That's the unrequested gift again, just disguised.

## Does this mean surprises are always a mistake?

No. The finding is about what people appreciate, on average, compared with what givers predict. A surprise from someone who knows the recipient very well can land.

The safer rule is narrower: when someone tells you what they want, believe them. Save surprises for people who have not told you, and for the extras around the gift.

## So what would I give?

Whatever they asked for, in the best version that fits the budget, with a short note saying it's from their list on purpose.

For a home cook who asked for a chef's knife, I'd give a hand-forged one from a small maker and set it on an engraved cutting board. The knife is their request. The board is the surprise, and it's small enough that it can't miss.

<!-- visual: callout | Short list: 4 ways to give the expected gift well, plus 1 skip -->

If a teen asks for the exact sneaker, the same earbuds as their friends, or just money, they're not being dull. Their tastes cluster around a few names, and the expected thing is often the right one. Here's the short list:

- **Give the exact thing.** The brand, model and color they named, not a near miss you think is more interesting.
- **If it's money, give it a job.** Name what it's for, in writing.
- **Put cash inside something they'll keep.** A slim leather wallet turns money into an object.
- **Add time.** A gift card plus a date to go shopping together.
- **Skip** the off-brand version of the thing they asked for. To a teen, it reads as a different gift.

## Why does every teen seem to want the same thing?

<!-- visual: chart | October 2025 teen survey: 87% own an iPhone; Nike No. 1 in clothing and footwear -->

Because their choices really are concentrated. Piper Sandler's Taking Stock With Teens survey, run with DECA, gathered input from 10,969 teens with an average age of 15.7, in results released in October 2025. Nike held the No. 1 spot for both clothing and footwear, and 87% of teens said they own an iPhone.

The same pattern shows up across categories. Piper Sandler's release names a short list of leaders: Amazon as the top shopping website among upper-income teens "by a wide margin," e.l.f. Cosmetics as the top cosmetics brand, and Sephora, Ulta and Target as the top beauty retailers.

To an adult, that looks like a lack of imagination. To a teen, the shared brand is the point. The gift fits into the world they're already in, rather than asking them to explain it to their friends.

## Why does the "boring" gift beat the creative one?

Because the creative one usually guesses, and the boring one doesn't. When a teen names a brand and model, they've already done the research, often more than you would.

Spending is also tighter than it looks. Piper Sandler found self-reported teen spending fell 6% year over year, to $2,213 a year. When teens are spending less of their own money, the thing they'd otherwise save up for is a gift that does real work.

So treat the request as a spec. If they asked for a particular pair of noise-cancelling headphones in black, that's the gift. A cheaper pair, a different color or a "better" brand you prefer is a different gift, and usually a lesser one to them.

## Is it lazy to give a teen money?

<!-- visual: callout | NRF 2025: gift cards 50%, clothing or accessories 46%, top of what people want -->

No, and the data is on their side. In the National Retail Federation's holiday survey of 8,247 adults in October 2025, gift cards were the top thing people said they wanted to receive, at 50%, ahead of clothing or accessories at 46%. That survey covered adults, not teens, but it shows how widely money-like gifts are welcomed.

Attitudes toward cash are warmer among younger people, too. An AP-NORC poll of 1,146 adults in December 2025 found about two-thirds of adults under 45 call cash a "very" acceptable holiday gift, compared with 55% of those 45 or older.

What makes money feel lazy isn't the money. It's the lack of anything around it.

## How do you give money without it feeling like a transaction?

Give it a story, a container or a plan.

- **A story.** Write what it's for: "for the concert in March," or "toward the bike." A specific purpose shows you listened.
- **A container.** A slim leather wallet with the cash or card already inside. The wallet lasts long after the money is spent, and a well-made one is a real gift at any age. A monogram makes it theirs.
- **A plan.** A gift card plus a set day to go together. The card covers the thing; the afternoon is the part they'll remember.

A small trick for younger teens: split it. Give part as the thing they asked for, and part as money with a named goal. They get the object and the choice.

## What if they asked for something you can't afford?

Give a clear share of it. A card that says "this covers half of the headphones you asked for" is more welcome than a different, cheaper item that solves nothing.

It also lets other family members chip in toward the same thing, rather than four people giving four near misses. One coordinated gift of the exact item tends to beat a pile of guesses.

If you'd still rather give an object, pick something the teen already uses every day and buy a better version of the exact type they like. Wireless earbuds and noise-cancelling headphones are classic examples: they get daily use, and it's worth asking which model they want. Our [gift ideas for teens](/guides/gift-ideas-for-teens/) have more in that vein.

## When is the creative gift the right call?

When you know the teen well enough to be right. A shared interest, an inside joke or a hobby you've watched them pursue for years can carry a surprise.

If you have to ask yourself whether they'll like it, that's your answer. Ask them, or a parent, and give what they name.

## So what would I give?

The exact thing they asked for, down to the color. If that's money, I'd put it inside a slim leather wallet with a note that says what it's for.

For a teen who wants a gadget everyone has, I'd give that gadget, in the model they named. It's not the boring choice. It's the one they picked.

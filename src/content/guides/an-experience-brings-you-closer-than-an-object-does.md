<!-- visual: callout | The finding in one line: experiences beat objects for closeness, even when the giver isn't there -->

Most gifts between partners are things. Research on what gifts do to a relationship suggests an experience does more, and that you don't have to be there for it to work. The short version:

- **The finding:** recipients of experiential gifts reported feeling closer to the giver than recipients of material gifts.
- **The surprise:** it held even when the recipient used the gift without the giver.
- **The reason:** experiences stirred stronger emotion when used, and that emotion drove the closeness.
- **The catch:** most people still give objects. In one survey, 78% had most recently given something material.
- **The workaround:** a physical gift framed around an experience gets some of the effect.

## Who studied this, and how?

<!-- visual: table | 4 studies: a small gift between friends, coffee mug framing, 534 recalled gifts, emotion test -->

Cindy Chan of the University of Toronto and Cassie Mogilner of UCLA Anderson published four studies in the *Journal of Consumer Research*, released online in December 2016 under the title "Experiential gifts foster stronger social relationships than material gifts."

They defined material gifts as objects to keep, and experiential gifts as events to live through. Then they measured something most gift research skips: not whether the recipient liked the gift, but whether the relationship changed.

In the first study, 59 pairs of friends came into a lab. One friend in each pair got a small sum of money to buy the other either an experience or a thing, and was told not to share it. A week later, recipients of experiences reported a bigger rise in closeness than recipients of objects. The experiences included a pass to a barre class and movie tickets. The objects included a shirt, a poster and a wine aerator.

## Do you need to be there for it to count?

<!-- visual: chart | Relationship change by gift type, shared vs not shared, from study 3 -->

No, and that's the part most people don't expect. The first study was built so the friends didn't share the gift, and 86% of recipients said they hadn't used it with the giver.

The third study tested sharing head-on. Of the 534 people whose answers were analyzed, those who recalled an experiential gift reported a bigger improvement in the relationship than those who recalled a material one. Whether they had used it with the giver made no significant difference. The largest share of the gifts, 37%, came from a spouse or significant other.

The authors' explanation is that the experience still sits inside the relationship. Your partner is moved at the concert, and the feeling attaches to the person who sent them.

## Why does an experience do this when an object doesn't?

Emotion during use, not at the unwrapping. In the third study, recipients rated how emotional they felt receiving the gift, and separately how emotional they felt using it. Experiences stirred more emotion during use. The fourth study tested whether that emotion was what drove the effect.

Chan, quoted in the University of Toronto's release, said experiential gifts are more socially connecting because they tend to be more emotionally evocative. Her examples were the awe of a safari, the excitement of a rock concert and the calm of a spa.

One more detail from the first study: recipients didn't rate experiences as significantly more thoughtful or better liked. The closeness effect wasn't about liking the gift more. It was about what the gift made them feel.

## Which experiences work best for a partner?

<!-- visual: checklist | 4 traits of a strong experience gift for a partner -->

The ones likely to stir real emotion, and easy to actually use. Chan's advice in the Toronto release was to start from their favorite hobby, or something new they've always wanted to try. She also told marketers to package experiences so the recipient isn't tied to one date and time, which is good advice for givers too.

A few traits to look for:

1. **It's felt, not just attended.** A live show, a class where they make something, a night somewhere new. Something they'll still talk about.
2. **It's flexible.** An open-dated class pass or voucher gets used. A fixed date can quietly expire.
3. **It suits them alone or together.** The research says you don't have to go. If they'd rather go with a friend, that's fine.
4. **It ends with something to keep, if possible.** A pottery or cooking class where they bring home what they made.

A small-studio pottery or cooking class works on all four counts.

## What if you'd rather give something to unwrap?

Give the object, and frame it around the experience it makes possible. The second study tested this directly. Two hundred givers handed a friend the same coffee mug. Half the mugs read "my coffee time," highlighting the experience. Half read "my coffee mug." Recipients of the "coffee time" mug reported a bigger boost in the relationship, while liking both mugs about the same.

So a pour-over coffee set, ideally with a hand-thrown dripper from a ceramics studio, given as "for our Sunday mornings," leans on the experience. A mini portable projector given with a note promising a monthly backyard movie night does the same. The object is the ticket; the card names the experience.

## So what would I give?

For a partner, a class in something they've wanted to try, at a small local studio, with an open date. Go with them if they want company. The research says it works either way.

If they need something to unwrap, I'd give a handmade pour-over set and write the experience on the card: the Sunday coffee you'll make together. It's still a thing, but the card turns it into a ritual.

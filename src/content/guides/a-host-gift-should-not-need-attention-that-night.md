The host is at the door, coat in one hand, timer going off in the kitchen. Whatever you hand over should be something they can put down and forget until tomorrow. Here's the short version:

- **Good:** a pantry gift for later, like a specialty olive oil or preserves.
- **Good:** a small entertaining piece, like a serving board or a platter, that goes straight on a shelf.
- **Good:** flowers already in a vase or a jar, so they're done the moment they're set down.
- **Needs attention:** a loose bouquet, which sends the host looking for a vase and scissors.
- **Comes with a question:** a bottle of wine, unless you say up front that it's for another night.

## What does the Emily Post Institute actually say?

<!-- visual: callout | 2 Emily Post lines: flowers best in a vase; don't expect your host to serve them -->

Two things that are easy to miss. First, flowers are welcome, but the Institute says a bouquet is "best brought in a vase (or mason jar) when possible." Second, consumables like wine, Champagne and chocolates are good gifts, but "don't expect your host to serve them; it's up to the host to decide."

Both lines come from the Institute's own advice page and its July 2025 "All about hosting gifts" column, signed Lizzie and Dan. Put together, they describe a gift that asks nothing of the host that night. The flowers are already arranged. The wine is a present, not a contribution to the menu.

## Why is a loose bouquet a problem?

Because it creates a task at the busiest moment of the evening. A bouquet in paper needs a vase, water, a trim and a spot on a crowded counter, all while guests are arriving and something is in the oven.

The Institute's fix is simple: bring it in a vase or a jar. The host can set it down as is. If you'd still rather bring a bouquet, a potted plant is another option the Institute mentions, as long as there's something to view. If the host has a cat, check our [housewarming and host gift guide](/guides/housewarming-and-host-gift-ideas/) for which plants the ASPCA lists as toxic.

## Why does wine raise a question?

Because the host has to decide, on the spot, whether you expect it poured. The Institute is clear that it's the host's call. A guest who doesn't know that can leave the host guessing.

You can take the question away with one sentence at the door: "This is for you, for another night." That turns a bottle into a gift instead of a menu item. The same goes for chocolates or a dessert you bring: say it's for later, and the host is off the hook.

## What can a host put down and enjoy tomorrow?

<!-- visual: table | 4 host gifts with why it needs nothing that night and who it suits -->

Anything that keeps, needs no setup and has an obvious place to go. The Institute's own list leans this way: specialty foods like a great olive oil or preserves, small entertaining items like little bowls, cutting boards and platters, cocktail napkins, picture frames and candles.

| Gift | Why it needs nothing that night | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| Specialty olive oil or preserves | Goes straight into the pantry | The host who cooks |
| Engraved serving board | Goes on a shelf, used at the next party | A first home, a couple who entertains |
| Artisan chocolate box | Keeps for days, opened when they like | Anyone, if you say it's for later |
| Flowers in a vase or jar | Already arranged, set down as is | A dinner party host |

Three kinds of gift fit best:

1. **A pantry gift from a small producer.** A bottle of estate olive oil or a set of small-batch preserves is something a cook uses for weeks. It's also easy to carry and impossible to mistake for a contribution to tonight's meal.
2. **A small entertaining piece.** An engraved serving board, with the family name or the house's street, works for the host who entertains often. It's used at the next party, which is a nice way to be remembered.
3. **A box of good chocolates.** The Institute lists chocolates with wine as a consumable gift. Choose a box from a small chocolatier, and say it's for them, not the table.

## When can you skip the host gift?

More often than you might think. The Institute recommends bringing a small gift the first time you visit someone's home. It also says casual dinners especially don't warrant a hosting gift, that close friends who dine together often can skip it, and that gifts aren't usually taken to large, formal dinners, especially if you don't know the host well.

It adds one more rule that keeps the whole thing calm: your gift should always be within your means. No one has to break their budget for a hosting gift.

## What else makes a host gift easy?

<!-- visual: callout | 3 habits: name on the tag, say it's for later, keep it small -->

Small habits that save the host a step:

- **Put your name on it.** A tag or a card means they know who to thank, even if they open it the next day.
- **Hand it over, then step back.** Don't wait for them to open it at the door.
- **Keep it small.** A host gift is a thank-you, not a housewarming present.

## So what would I give?

For a dinner party, I'd bring a bottle of small-producer olive oil or a set of preserves, with a tag that says "for your pantry, not tonight." It goes on the counter, then in the cupboard, and the host never has to think about it during the evening.

For a host I know well who entertains often, I'd give an engraved serving board. And if I wanted to bring flowers, I'd bring them in a jar, as the Emily Post Institute suggests, so they're finished the moment they're set down.

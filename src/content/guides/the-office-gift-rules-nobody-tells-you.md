<!-- visual: callout | Short list: 4 office gift rules (down not up, group gifts optional, keep peers equal, check policy) -->

Office gifts have rules, and almost nobody explains them. The short version:

- **Gifts flow down, not up.** A manager can give to the team. The team doesn't owe the manager anything.
- **A gift for the boss comes from the group,** and joining is optional, with no one tracking who gave what.
- **Between peers, keep it small and even,** or keep it out of the office.
- **Check the policy.** Some employers cap what you can give or accept, and federal employees have a written limit.

## Why do office gifts flow down?

<!-- visual: chart | Gift direction: manager to team OK, team to manager discouraged, group gift optional -->

Because of power. Alison Green of Ask a Manager puts it plainly: "gifts in a workplace should flow downward, not upward." Gifts from bosses to employees are fine; employees shouldn't be expected to give to those above them.

Her reason is the relationship itself. People shouldn't feel obligated to buy gifts for someone with power over their livelihood, she writes, and managers should never benefit from that power in this way.

The Emily Post Institute reaches the same place from a different side. Its corporate gift page says: "Don't give a gift to your supervisor that's just from you." Other employees may resent what they see as an effort to curry favor with the boss.

## Is the rule ever written down?

Yes, for U.S. federal workers, and it's more specific than most people expect. The federal ethics rules (5 CFR 2635.304) allow gifts to an official superior only in narrow cases. On an occasional basis, such as a holiday, an employee may give items other than cash with an aggregate value of $10 or less per occasion, or food and refreshments shared in the office among several employees.

Larger gifts are allowed for infrequent occasions of personal significance, such as a marriage, an illness, a bereavement or the birth or adoption of a child, and for occasions that end the working relationship, such as a retirement or a transfer. A birthday doesn't count: one of the rule's own examples says a 50th birthday isn't an infrequent occasion.

A private employer may have no written rule at all. But the federal version is a useful map of where the lines fall: small and shared is fine, big moments are fine, and an expensive personal present for the boss is not.

## How do you run a group gift without pressure?

<!-- visual: callout | 4 steps for an optional group gift: deadline, anonymous envelope, suggested amount, everyone signs the card -->

Make it anonymous and optional. The Emily Post Institute says the best way to give to a boss is as a department or group, and it lays out a method:

1. **Set a deadline.**
2. **Keep an envelope at your desk** for anonymous contributions.
3. **Suggest an amount if you like,** but make clear that the amount, and even participation, is entirely up to each person.
4. **Buy the gift with what comes in.**

The anonymous envelope is the part people skip, and it's the part that matters. Nobody can tell who gave a little and who gave nothing.

If you're on the other side of the ask, Alison Green says you should never feel pressured into spending money you can't afford or don't want to spend. "I'm sorry, I can't participate this year" is enough. She also suggests proposing a card, or treats for everyone, instead of a gift for the boss.

## What should a manager do?

Say something early. Green suggests that a manager tell the team, at the start of the season, that good work is gift enough and that nobody should have to give to their boss. If a small gift arrives anyway, she says to accept it graciously, so the point is about protecting people's money, not making anyone feel bad.

Giving down is the easy half. A manager can give each person on the team the same thing, so nobody compares.

## What about gifts between coworkers?

<!-- visual: table | 3 safe peer gifts and 4 to avoid, from Ask a Manager and the Emily Post Institute -->

Keep them equal, small and impersonal. Green warns against creating obligations for people who might not have the budget, or the inclination, to reciprocate. Offices that handle it well, she says, limit exchanges, such as a swap where each person brings a single gift.

The Emily Post Institute says you can give to your closest colleagues, but discreetly, preferably outside the office, and to keep the gift simple and moderately priced.

| Usually safe | Usually too personal |
|---|---|
| Food to share with the group | Perfume or cologne |
| A gift certificate to a coffee shop or bookstore | Body and skin care |
| Cards | Pajamas or jewelry |
| | Political or religious items |

Both sources point to food. Green notes that food can be shared with the group, which sidesteps the politics of individual gifts.

## Does my company have its own limit?

Possibly, and it's worth checking before you buy. The Emily Post Institute notes that many companies forbid any corporate gift giving, or only allow employees to accept gifts valued less than $25, and that some require gifts above that to be disclosed. Those rules are mostly about clients, but they're a sign that HR may have a view. If you're giving a significant gift to a client, the Institute says to call that company's HR department first.

## So what would I give?

For a manager giving to the team, I'd give everyone the same gourmet snack box, or a bookstore gift card, which matches the Institute's suggestion of a gift certificate to a bookstore. Same gift, every desk, no ranking.

For a boss who's retiring, which the federal rules treat as a special occasion, I'd organize a group gift through an anonymous envelope and buy something personal and lasting, such as an engraved pen. For a regular holiday, I'd skip the boss entirely and bring treats for the whole office. Shared food is the one gift all three sources make room for.

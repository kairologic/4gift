<!-- visual: callout | Short list: 3 kinds of gift for a downsizer, plus 1 to skip -->

For a parent, grandparent or friend who is moving somewhere smaller, the gift that lands is the one that doesn't need a shelf. Here's the short list:

- **Gets used up:** a box of artisan chocolate, good tea or coffee, anything that is gone by spring.
- **Replaces a worse version:** an e-reader in place of a wall of paperbacks, or one photo book in place of a carton of prints.
- **Gives time:** a dated, written promise of a day spent sorting, hauling or driving to the donation center.
- **Skip** anything decorative, anything in a set, and anything they would feel guilty giving away.

## How much stuff do older adults say they have?

<!-- visual: chart | 2010 HRS, ages 60+: 60% more things than they need, 35% just right, 5% fewer -->

More than they want. In early data from the 2010 Health and Retirement Study, 60% of people 60 and older said they have more things than they need. Another 35% said just the right amount, and only 5% said fewer.

The figures come from David Ekerdt of the University of Kansas, who wrote up the early-release data (1,089 respondents age 60 and older living in the community) in a 2012 newsletter for Rutgers' Center for State Health Policy. The wealthiest fifth of respondents were the most likely to say they had too much: 75% of them.

So when you give a downsizer one more object, odds are you are adding to a pile they already think is too big.

## Why is it so hard to let things go?

<!-- visual: table | Past year, ages 60+: cleaned out not at all 26%, sold nothing 80%, gave nothing to family or friends 34%, gave nothing to charity 27% -->

Because getting rid of things takes work that most people don't get to. In the same data, 26% had not cleaned out at all in the past year, 80% had sold nothing, 34% gave nothing to family or friends, and 27% gave nothing to charity.

Ekerdt's term for this is the "material convoy": the caravan of possessions that follows a person through life. In a 2020 KU News piece about his book, *Downsizing: Confronting Our Possessions in Later Life* (Columbia University Press), he describes that convoy as basically unsustainable once someone needs to move to a smaller place. His team interviewed more than 100 people going through the process, and he says many of them called it one of the hardest things they have ever done.

Wanting less is not the same as having less. People who said they had too many things were only marginally more active at clearing them out, Ekerdt found. A gift can help close that gap, or widen it.

## What kind of gift doesn't add to the pile?

<!-- visual: callout | 3 tests: is it gone in a few months, does it replace two things, does it come with a date -->

One that passes at least one of three tests.

1. **It gets used up.** Food, drink, a candle they will burn. In a few months it has left the house on its own.
2. **It replaces more than it adds.** One good thing that lets two or ten worse things go.
3. **It is time, not a thing.** Help with the move, a meal on moving day, a ride.

A consumable is the easiest. A box of artisan chocolate from a small maker, or a tea or coffee they would not buy for themselves, is a real gift with an end date. For someone moving into a place with a smaller kitchen, that matters more than a new gadget would.

## What should replace the things they're giving up?

The one object that stands in for many. Two work especially well.

An **e-reader** replaces bookshelves. For a reader leaving a house with a den full of paperbacks, it means the books can go to a library sale without the reading going with them. Load a few titles they have mentioned before you hand it over, and set the type size they like.

A **custom photo book** replaces boxes. Ask for the carton of loose prints, scan the best of them, and give back one bound book. The prints can then go to the relatives who want them, and the move gets one box lighter. It is also a keepsake a downsizer can keep without guilt, because it took the place of something bigger.

The test is honest subtraction. If you can't name what your gift lets them get rid of, it is probably adding to the pile.

## How do you give help so it actually gets used?

<!-- visual: callout | 28% told to downsize, and half of those were offered help -->

Put a date and a task on it. "I'll come Saturday the 14th and we'll do the garage" gets used. "Let me know if you need anything" usually doesn't.

Ekerdt's data has a telling detail: 28% of people 60 and older had been told by family or friends to downsize, and only half of those said the others offered to help. Advice is common. A pair of hands is rarer.

Useful versions of time:

- **A sorting day,** with you doing the lifting and the trips to donate.
- **The selling,** since 80% sold nothing: you photograph and list the furniture they want gone.
- **Moving-day dinner,** delivered to the new place while the kitchen boxes are still taped.
- **A drive around the new neighborhood,** to find the pharmacy, grocery and library before the move.

Reluctance is real: 48% said they would be very reluctant to move, in the same data. Go at their pace, and let them decide what stays.

## So what would I give?

For a reader, I'd give an e-reader with five books already on it, and an offer to drive the paperbacks to the library sale. For someone with a closet full of photos, I'd give a custom photo book made from their own prints.

For anyone else, a box of good chocolate and a card with a date on it: the day you'll come and help. The chocolate will be gone in a week. The help is the part they're likely to remember.

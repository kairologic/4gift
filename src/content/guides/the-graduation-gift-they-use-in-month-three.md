<!-- visual: callout | Short list: 3 ways to give for month three, plus 1 skip -->

Cash is the default graduation gift, and the default is mostly right. The trick is to give it for month three, when the party is over and the real costs show up. Here's the short list:

- **Give money a job.** Call it an emergency fund, and say so on the card.
- **Pair it with one daily-use object.** Noise-cancelling headphones for a commute, or a good chef's knife for a first kitchen.
- **Add a check-in.** A note to open in three months, or a dinner on the calendar.
- **Skip** the gift that only works on graduation day, like a decoration or a novelty with the year on it.

## What do most people give a graduate?

<!-- visual: callout | NRF 2026: record $7.2 billion, 39% buying, cash the top gift -->

Cash. The National Retail Federation's 2026 graduation survey found that cash is the most popular gift again this year. NRF expects graduation spending to reach a record $7.2 billion, and 39% of respondents plan to buy a graduation gift. The survey reached 7,914 adults from April 30 to May 6.

That makes cash the safe choice. It isn't the most thoughtful-looking one, and that's what this piece is about: how to give the default so that it lands when it's needed.

## What does a new graduate run short of?

<!-- visual: chart | Fed SHED: share who'd cover a $400 emergency with cash, ages 18 to 29 vs all adults, 2021 to 2025 -->

A cushion. The Federal Reserve asks adults every year whether they could cover a $400 emergency expense using cash or its equivalent. In its 2025 data, 45% of adults ages 18 to 29 said yes. For all adults, the figure was 63%.

The younger group has also slipped. The Fed's table shows 58% of 18 to 29 year olds could cover the $400 in 2021, then 52% in 2023, 47% in 2024 and 45% in 2025.

That's the gap a graduation gift can fill. In the first months of a job or a new city, the costs that hurt are the unplanned ones: a car repair, a vet bill, a deposit, a flight home. A graduate who has a few hundred dollars set aside can absorb one of those. One who doesn't may have to borrow.

## How do you make cash feel like more than cash?

Give it a name and a time. "Spend it on whatever you like" is fine. "This is the start of your emergency fund. Don't touch it until something breaks" is a gift with a plan.

A few ways to frame it:

- **The emergency fund.** Write the purpose on the card. The Fed's figures give you a good reason to: fewer than half of adults under 30 had that cushion in 2025.
- **The first-apartment fund.** For the graduate moving out, money earmarked for the move and the first month's surprises.
- **The bridge.** For the graduate starting a job, money that covers the stretch before the first full paycheck.

If you want it to feel like an object too, put it in an envelope inside a good slim leather wallet, and write the job on the envelope.

## What should you give if not cash?

<!-- visual: table | 2 daily-use gifts for month three, who each suits, and when to skip -->

One thing they'll use every day once the new routine starts. The test is simple: will it get used on an ordinary Tuesday in month three?

| Gift | Best for | Why it lasts | Skip it if |
|---|---|---|---|
| Noise-cancelling headphones | Commuters, open offices, shared apartments | Used on every train ride and every focus hour | They already own a pair they like |
| Chef's knife | A graduate with a first kitchen | The one tool a new cook uses for nearly every meal | They're still on a meal plan |

Noise-cancelling headphones are the gift I'd pick for a graduate heading into a commute or an open-plan office. A shared apartment is loud, too.

A good chef's knife suits the graduate moving into a first place with a kitchen. It's the tool a new cook reaches for at nearly every meal, so a sharp, well-balanced one earns its place. A knife made by a small maker, or engraved with their initials, makes it a keepsake as well as a tool.

## Is there a graduation gift to skip?

Yes. Anything that is only good for the day itself. A dated decoration or a novelty gift gets one photo and then a drawer.

Also skip a big gadget they didn't ask for. An expensive surprise can feel like one more thing to set up and manage, at a time when everything is new.

## When should you give it?

At graduation, with a second touch later. A card at the ceremony with the money and its job, then a short message, or a dinner, around month three.

By then the party is long over, and the new routine is real. A check-in at that point says you remember the hard part, not only the cap and gown.

## So what would I give?

Cash, with a job: the start of an emergency fund, written on the card. The Fed's numbers say that's what many young adults are missing.

If I wanted an object as well, I'd add noise-cancelling headphones for the commuter or a good chef's knife for the graduate with a first kitchen. And I'd put a dinner on the calendar for month three.
